Theory Foundations

Before you touch the guitar,
learn to speak its language.

Eight short lessons and a quiz to get you ready for your first class with Gabriel — no instrument required yet, just a few minutes and some curiosity.

01 — The Musical Alphabet

Only seven letters. Then it loops.

Western music runs on just seven letter names: A, B, C, D, E, F, G. Once you reach G, the alphabet doesn't stop — it loops right back around to A, one octave higher.

Between most letters sits a sharp (♯) or flat (♭) note, giving you a note "in between." But two pairs sit right next to each other with nothing in between at all:

B → C and E → F have no sharp or flat between them. Everywhere else, there's a black key waiting in the middle.
02 — The Fingerboard Grid

One fret, one half step. Two frets, one whole step.

The guitar makes distance visual. Every fret you move is a half step. Skip two frets and you've moved a whole step.

That's the whole grid. Once half steps and whole steps click, you can measure any distance on the neck just by counting frets — no guessing required.

Half step = 1 fret. Whole step = 2 frets. Everything else in theory is built from these two distances.
03 — The Major Scale

One formula. Every major scale.

Every major scale — in any key, on any instrument — follows the exact same recipe of whole (W) and half (H) steps:

W – W – H – W – W – W – H

Start on any note and apply that formula, and you'll land on a major scale. Here it is starting from C — which happens to use no sharps or flats at all.

04 — Finding the Root

Every chord and key has an anchor.

The root is the note a chord or key is named after — the note everything else is built on and pulls back toward. In a G major chord, G is the root. In the key of D, D is the root.

Once you can spot the root by ear or on the neck, you can navigate any chord or key even before you know every note inside it.

Find the root first. Everything else in the chord is measured relative to it.
05 — Rhythm: The Pulse of Music

Playing the right notes isn't enough. You need the right timing too.

Different note types tell you how long a sound should last, measured in beats:

  • Whole note — 4 beats. Count "1‑2‑3‑4," ringing through the whole bar.
  • Half note — 2 beats. Count "1‑2."
  • Quarter note — 1 beat. One note played on every beat.
  • Eighth note — 1/2 beat. Count "1‑&‑2‑&‑3‑&‑4‑&," two notes per beat.
Same bar, four ways to divide it — the diagram shows how one whole note equals two halves, four quarters, or eight eighths.
06 — Reading the Staff

Five lines, four spaces, two ways to read them.

Standard music is written on a staff — five horizontal lines and four spaces. Which "clef" you read depends on your instrument: guitar players read Treble, bass players read Bass.

  • Treble lines (bottom to top): E‑G‑B‑D‑F — Every Good Boy Does Fine
  • Treble spaces: F‑A‑C‑E — spells FACE
  • Bass lines (bottom to top): G‑B‑D‑F‑A — Good Boys Do Fine Always
  • Bass spaces: A‑C‑E‑G — All Cows Eat Grass
The mnemonics are there so you never have to count lines from scratch — just remember the phrase.
07 — What Is a Chord?

One note is a melody. Several at once is harmony.

Playing a single note at a time gives you a melody — a line you could hum. Play three or more different notes at the exact same time, and you get a chord.

Chords are the engine that drives a song forward. They're what a singer or soloist leans on underneath the melody.

A chord = three or more different notes, played together, at the same time.
08 — Major vs. Minor Chords

Same root, one note apart, totally different mood.

Major chords sound bright, happy, and resolved — think of the triumphant strum at the end of a rock song.

Minor chords sound dark, sad, or moody. Major and minor chords share the same root — but take the middle note of a major chord and lower it by just one half step (one fret), and the whole emotion shifts.

One fret on the middle note is the entire difference between "happy" and "sad."
